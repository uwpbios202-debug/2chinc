8135 S Stony Island Ave, Chicago: Build Scope

Prepared 10/29/2026 for the owner by 2nd Chance Housing Inc. A planning checklist, not architectural or engineering drawings and not legal advice. Code figures below are from general knowledge of the 2010 ADA Standards, the Illinois Accessibility Code and Chicago's codes and were not checked against the current text in this session. The architect must confirm every number and citation.

Goal (updated 10/29/2026): a gutted two-story building (down to studs, no services, no walls or doors) becomes a kitchen in the former brick garage on the first floor, dining on the first and second floors, and a rooftop summer party area with amplified music. Basement: storage and all mechanical equipment. Walls and roof: structural insulated panels (SIPs). The budget (materials and labor) is in the companion file 8135-S-Stony-Island-material-costs.html.

1. Team and approvals

  • Licensed Illinois architect (assembly use needs sealed drawings) and a structural engineer. MEP engineer for mechanical, electrical and plumbing, and a fire protection designer.
  • Zoning check: confirm the district allows a restaurant with rooftop use, outdoor seating, any liquor service and the parking count. Ask whether a special use or variance is needed.
  • Permits: Chicago building permit (full rehab, likely the Standard Plan Review track for this size), plus separate trades permits. A permit expediter is worth the cost.
  • Health: Chicago Department of Public Health plan review for a retail food establishment, before construction of the kitchen.
  • Licenses before opening: retail food, business, liquor if wanted, and any public-place-of-amusement or rooftop license the City requires. Confirm with the architect and the Dept. of Business Affairs.
  • Environmental: asbestos and lead survey before any further demolition, and check for underground tanks given the former garage.
  • Insurance and builder's risk in place before work starts. Contractor must be licensed, bonded and insured.

2. Structure and envelope

  • Structural assessment of floors, roof, masonry, foundation and the joint where the old garage was incorporated.
  • Second-floor dining: floor framing sized for assembly live load (code uses about 100 psf; engineer confirms).
  • Rooftop party area: the roof is not a deck today. It needs engineered structure for assembly loading, new waterproofing and drainage, and a code-compliant guard at least 42 in. high around the entire edge. Add the load of planters, tents, kitchen carts and crowd.
  • Masonry repair and tuckpointing, new roof membrane, insulation to the Chicago Energy Transformation Code, new windows and exterior doors, lintels where openings are cut.
  • Foundation drainage and basement waterproofing, sump if needed.

3. Accessibility: whole building (ADA 2010 Standards and Illinois Accessibility Code)

When two codes differ, the stricter one governs. Illinois's code is often stricter than the ADA on details.

4. Restrooms (ADA compliant)

  • Fixture count: set by the Chicago Plumbing Code from the occupant load of each floor. Expect restrooms on each public floor, and one near the rooftop if it will hold events.
  • Single-user, all-gender restrooms are the simplest way to be accessible (Chicago has an all-gender single-user restroom rule; the architect should confirm how it applies here). Design each as a fully accessible room.
  • Door: 32 in. clear, swings out or has a clear 60 in. turning space inside, pull side 18 in. latch-side clearance, lever hardware, lock operable with a closed fist.
  • Room: 60 in. turning circle clear of door swing and fixtures.
  • Water closet: centerline 16 to 18 in. from the side wall; seat 17 to 19 in. high; flush control on the open side, 48 in. maximum high.
  • Grab bars: 1-1/4 to 2 in. diameter, 33 to 36 in. above the floor, 36 in. long behind the toilet starting at most 12 in. from the side wall, 42 in. long on the side wall starting at most 12 in. from the back wall. Walls reinforced to hold 250 lb.
  • Lavatory: top at most 34 in., knee clearance 27 in. high, 30 by 48 in. clear space, insulated pipes, faucet operable without twisting or pinching. Mirror bottom edge at most 40 in.
  • Accessories: dispensers, towel and waste at most 40 in. (reach 15 to 48 in.). Paper towel or air dryer clear of the route.
  • If a separate men's and women's room is used instead: each needs an accessible stall (60 in. wide minimum) and at least one ambulatory stall as the code requires, plus accessible urinals if any.
  • Staff restroom: an accessible one as well. Plan a mop sink and employee hand sinks.
  • Drain, vent and cold and hot supply sized to the fixture count. Floor drain and trap primer where code requires.

5. Services (everything is gone: start from the street)

5a. Water and sewer

  • New water service line and meter (Chicago Dept. of Water Management approval). Size from the fixture-unit count and sprinkler demand. A restaurant plus sprinkler often needs a larger service than a house.
  • Backflow preventer, shut-offs, hot-water heating (restaurant demand; consider a high-efficiency tank-type plus a booster at the dish machine).
  • New sanitary building drain and connection to the City sewer. Camera the old lines first; replace what fails.
  • Grease interceptor sized for the kitchen (Chicago requires one for food service; location outside or in the basement for access). Floor sinks and indirect drains for kitchen equipment.
  • Roof and area drains per code. Rooftop drains should have overflow scuppers.

5b. Electric

  • New service from ComEd. Call early for the load letter and service type; a restaurant usually needs three-phase power and a larger service than a house.
  • Meter and main disconnect, then distribution panels or a load center on each floor, sized by the electrical engineer from the kitchen equipment list.
  • Lighting, receptacles (GFCI where code requires), dedicated circuits for equipment, exit signs and emergency lighting, and battery backup for life-safety systems.
  • Rooftop: weatherproof receptacles, lighting, and safe wiring for events. Data and security conduit everywhere.

5c. Gas

  • New gas service and meter from Peoples Gas for the kitchen range, fryers and heating. Gas shut-off interlocked with the hood fire-suppression system, and any gas detection the code requires.

5d. Heating, cooling and ventilation

  • Load calculation for each floor. Rooftop units or split systems, with fresh air for the dining areas sized to the occupant load.
  • Kitchen: Type I exhaust hood with fire-suppression system over cooking equipment, make-up air unit, grease duct to the roof with the required clearances.
  • Restroom exhaust, dish room exhaust, walk-in cooler condensers.

5e. Fire and life safety

  • Automatic sprinklers throughout (Chicago generally requires them for an assembly use of this kind), with a fire pump if the water pressure needs it, and a fire department connection.
  • Fire alarm with smoke detection, pull stations, horns and strobes, and monitoring.
  • Two or more exits from the first floor, second floor and roof, with enclosed stair or stairs sized to the occupant load, exit signs and illuminated paths.
  • Kitchen: portable extinguishers, hood suppression tied to the gas shut-off.

6. First floor: kitchen and dining

  • Layout: receiving door and dry storage, walk-in cooler and freezer, prep, cook line under the hood, dish room, pass to the dining room, staff restroom and lockers, office.
  • Kitchen finishes the health department requires: smooth, nonabsorbent, easily cleaned floors, walls and ceilings (quarry tile or sealed concrete with a cove base, FRP or tile walls, washable ceiling).
  • Hand sinks, three-compartment sink, mop sink, dish machine, ice machine with air gap, grease interceptor, floor sinks.
  • Dining room with accessible tables and bar if planned. Host stand. Accessible route from the entrance to every part.
  • The old brick garage becomes the kitchen. Check its structure, slab level and slope to drains, wall condition, ceiling height under the hood, and that it sits beside the dining room, the receiving door and the basement stair.

7. Second floor: dining room

  • Enclosed stair, two exits, and a service stair or dumbwaiter so food reaches the second floor.
  • Single-user restrooms, a service station and a servery. The first floor must carry the fully accessible restrooms, tables and bar. Whether the second-floor restrooms must also be accessible is an architect question; this list prices them as accessible, which costs little extra.
  • Acoustic ceiling and walls. Plan for noise toward neighbors. Windows to the street or lot.
  • Fire-rated walls and doors where the code separates the stair and mechanical rooms.

8. Roof: summer party area

  • Stair access from the second floor, guard at 42 in. around the entire edge, a second means of egress if the occupant load calls for it. Amplified music: check Chicago noise limits and hours, neighbors and speaker placement; plan a sound-rated parapet, speaker zones and a sound limiter.
  • Structure and waterproofing as in Section 2. Non-slip, drained pavers or decking over a protected membrane.
  • Occupant load set by the architect. Enclosed stair head house.
  • Restrooms or a restroom trailer location, bar and service area, lighting, power, plumbing for a bar sink, speakers within City noise rules, trash.
  • Check any limit the City or the zoning district puts on rooftop use, and liquor and noise rules if the party use includes alcohol and amplified sound.

9. Site: lots, parking and trees

  • Survey and parcel lines. Decide whether the lots stay separate.
  • Tree clearing: count and size the trees. Check City rules for work near the public way and any tree removal permit. Get a quote per tree and for stump grinding and hauling.
  • North lot: grade, stormwater (Chicago's stormwater rules apply to larger land disturbance), pavement, striping, accessible spaces, wheel stops, lighting, a trash enclosure, landscaping and a fence along the street per zoning.
  • South yard: fence, possible outdoor dining patio (accessible), loading area, and screening for rooftop equipment.
  • Sidewalks and curb ramps in the public way must be accessible. Any work in the public way needs its own permit.

10. Finishes and equipment (owner's selections)

  • Interior walls and doors (all missing): framing, drywall, doors and hardware to ADA reach and force limits, fire-rated where required.
  • Floors, ceilings, paint, trim, millwork, bar. Furniture, fixtures and equipment. Kitchen equipment list drives the gas, electric, ventilation and plumbing sizes, so choose it early.
  • Signage, exterior lighting, security cameras, point-of-sale and network.

11. Suggested order of work

  1. Measure and survey. Pull permit history. Environmental survey. Zoning review.
  2. Hire the architect and engineers. Decide the concept, the occupant loads and the equipment list.
  3. Structural fixes. Roof structure for the party deck.
  4. Apply for utility services (water, ComEd, Peoples Gas) at the same time as permit drawings. Lead times are long.
  5. Permit and Health review. Start rough-in once permits issue.
  6. Rough plumbing, electric, gas, HVAC, sprinkler, fire alarm. Inspections.
  7. Insulation, drywall, kitchen finishes, hood and equipment.
  8. Restrooms, finishes, site work and parking.
  9. Final inspections, Health inspection, licenses, certificate of occupancy.

12. Questions for the architect

  • Total occupant load by floor and roof, and the exit count that follows.
  • Required fixture count and whether single-user all-gender restrooms satisfy it.
  • Sprinkler and fire pump need. Water service size.
  • Is the rooftop use allowed by zoning, and what licenses apply?
  • Stormwater and parking rules for the north lot.
  • Rough budget range per section and a realistic schedule.

No costs or timelines are stated because nothing here has been measured or bid. Section numbers can be copied into a contractor's bid form so every bidder prices the same scope.
