Module A · Why this work matters · 8 of 110

A death can leave a house in limbo.

Still in her name

The deed may still name the person who died. Taxes, insurance, repairs and rent do not stop. The family may be unable to sell, refinance or prove who owns it.

A process they do not know

Words like letters, heirship and administration are unfamiliar. Families are grieving and often do not know where to start or whom to trust.

Costs add up

Lawyers are paid for their time. When a family arrives without documents, names and answers, the first hours go to gathering and explaining.

Remember: the deed is one lead. Do not assume the family is in trouble, that probate is needed, or that anyone is entitled to anything. Your job is to understand the facts.

Your role: find, ask, gather, organize.

You do, with permission

  • Find possible cases from public records and obituaries.
  • Ask the family respectfully, and only with their permission.
  • Gather documents and facts, with the source for each.
  • Enter what you know into the preparation system; leave blanks as blanks.
  • Write down every unanswered question.
  • Assemble and check the package, and hand it to counsel.

Counsel, the court or another professional does

  • Advise the family and choose the route.
  • Decide who the legal heirs are and their shares.
  • Draft, correct, approve, sign and file court papers.
  • Give legal advice, appear in court, and handle the title company's legal questions.
  • Approve the package. An internal checkbox is never counsel's approval.

Counsel decides We never say counsel “just uploads the documents.” Counsel reviews, corrects, approves and files.

What an organized case saves counsel.

Without preparation

  • The same questions asked again in every meeting.
  • Missing documents found one at a time.
  • Two documents that disagree, noticed late.
  • No list of what is still unknown.
  • Hours spent decoding what the family meant.

With a checked package

  • One interview; answers saved in one place.
  • An evidence index: what exists, where, and its source.
  • Names, dates, PINs and shares compared across documents first.
  • An unresolved-questions sheet, so counsel sees the gaps.
  • A short cover memo that points to the next decision.
Honest goal: a well-supported, internally consistent package that minimizes counsel's preparation work. It does not remove counsel's review, corrections or approval.

Twelve words, in plain language. (1 of 2)

Probate
A court process that deals with the property of a person who has died: proving a will if there is one, naming who may act, paying debts, and passing on what is left. Not every case needs it.
Decedent
The person who has died.
Heir
A person the law says may inherit when there is no will. Counsel confirms who the heirs are and their shares.
Beneficiary
A person named to receive something: in a will, a trust, or a transfer-on-death instrument.
Administrator
The person a court appoints to manage an estate when there is no will.
Executor
The person a will names to manage an estate; the court must still appoint them.
Module A · Why this work matters · 12 of 110

Twelve words, in plain language. (2 of 2)

Letters of office
The court's certificate that a person has authority to act for an estate. Banks and others ask to see it. Some routes never need them; counsel decides.
Deed
A recorded document that transfers ownership of real estate.
Legal description
The exact wording that identifies a parcel (lots, blocks, section, township). Copy it exactly from the recorded deed. An address is not a legal description.
PIN
The property index number: the 14-digit parcel number used by Cook County. A property can have more than one. Every PIN is searched separately.
Lien
A claim recorded against property for a debt, such as a mortgage or a judgment. A lien in the records is a fact to report, not a conclusion that money is owed.
Certified copy
A copy issued and certified by the office that keeps the record. A photocopy, or a copy a notary stamps, is not certified.

In person: a short, gentle first visit.

At the door

“Hello, I'm [name] from 2nd Chance Housing, a nonprofit. I'm sorry to bother you. I'm looking for family of [Delia Marsh], who I understand may have passed. I'm not selling anything and I'm not a lawyer. May I leave you my card, or may I tell you in two minutes why I'm here?”

Do

  • Go in daylight, with an ID badge or card.
  • Speak from the public walk or door; wait to be invited in.
  • Leave a card or a short written note (see the next slides for email-first follow-up) if no one is home.
  • Ask who the best contact person is, and how they prefer to be reached.

Do not

  • Do not touch, collect or read mail, notices or papers.
  • Do not look in windows, enter, or ask a neighbor to open anything.
  • Do not mention money, or that the property “is in trouble.”
  • Do not return again and again if told no.

Speak with grief in the room.

Say

  • “I'm so sorry for your loss.”
  • “Tell me about her, if you'd like.” Then listen.
  • “We can go as slowly as you need.”
  • “We can stop for today.”

Avoid

  • “You need to act fast.”
  • “I know how you feel.”

What you must never do or say.

Never imply or promise

  • That you are a court, county, city or government worker.
  • That counsel will be free, or any lawyer will take the case.
  • That the family owns the property, or will qualify for anything.
  • That a result is guaranteed, or that it will be quick.
  • That the family must act, sign or pay now.

Never do

  • Pressure, repeat visits after a no, or contact people who asked not to be contacted.
  • Enter the property, or go on private grounds without invitation.
  • Collect or open someone's mail, or ask someone to.
  • Use a deceased person's logins, cards or accounts.
  • Ask anyone for payment, or take gifts.
  • Say anything about the case to anyone who has not been authorized by the family.
If you slip: correct it plainly and tell your supervisor the same day. Honesty protects the family, the case and you.

The first interview, in a sensible order.

  1. Consent first. Review and sign the consent. Only then collect.
  2. The person who died. Full name, other names, date and place of death, where she lived.
  3. The property. Address, every PIN you know, who lives there, who pays what.
  4. Paper that exists. Will, trust, deed, death certificate, notices, bills. Who holds each original.
  5. The family. Spouse or partner, children, anyone who died, anyone hard to reach.
  6. What they want. Keep the home, share it, sell, refinance: their goal, not your advice.

Second-person review: compare, do not just count.

A good review

  • Done by someone other than the preparer.
  • Reads each document against the evidence and against the other documents.
  • Looks for contradictions, not just for missing files.
  • Writes down every finding: what, where, and who must fix it.
  • Signs and dates the review sheet. The preparer fixes; the reviewer re-checks.

A weak review

  • “All the files are there.”
  • A checkmark with no comparison.
  • The same person who wrote the petition.
  • A review that changes facts without evidence to support it.
Why: the biggest errors are not missing papers. They are two papers that say different things: a misspelled name, a missing PIN, a share that does not add up, a date that does not match.
