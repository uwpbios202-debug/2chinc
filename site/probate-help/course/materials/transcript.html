Welcome. This is self guided training for researchers and family support volunteers at Second Chance Housing. You will learn how to find a family who may need help after a relative has died, how to ask for permission, how to gather and check the facts, how to use our preparation system, and how to hand a lawyer a complete package to review. You can learn at your own pace. Press play, or use the arrows.

Open slide 6

7. Why this work matters

Module A. Why this work matters. In this short module you will see the problem a family faces, what you do and do not do, and the words you will hear again and again.

Open slide 7

8. A death can leave a house in limbo.

A death can leave a house in limbo. The deed may still name the person who died. Taxes, insurance, repairs and rent do not stop, and the family may not be able to sell, refinance or prove who owns the property. The process is unfamiliar, the family is grieving, and a lawyer's time costs money. Notice that we do not assume anyone is in trouble or that probate is needed. Your job is to understand the facts.

Open slide 8

9. Your role: find, ask, gather, organize.

Your role is to find, ask, gather and organize. With permission, you find possible cases, ask the family respectfully, gather documents and facts with the source for each, enter what you know into the system, write down every unanswered question, and hand counsel an organized package. Counsel, the court or another professional advises the family, chooses the route, decides who the legal heirs are, and drafts, approves and files the papers. We never say that counsel just uploads documents. Counsel always reviews.

Open slide 9

10. What an organized case saves counsel.

What does an organized case save counsel? Without preparation, the same questions are asked in every meeting, missing documents turn up one at a time, and two documents that disagree are noticed late. With a checked package, there is one interview, an evidence index, names dates and parcel numbers compared across documents, a sheet of unresolved questions, and a short cover memo. The honest goal is a well supported, internally consistent package that minimizes counsel's preparation work. It never removes counsel's review.

Open slide 10

11. Twelve words, in plain language. (1 of 2)

Twelve words in plain language. First six. Probate is a court process that deals with the property of a person who has died. Not every case needs it. The decedent is the person who has died. An heir is a person the law says may inherit when there is no will; counsel confirms who the heirs are. A beneficiary is a person named to receive something in a will, a trust or a transfer on death instrument. An administrator is appointed by the court when there is no will. An executor is named in a will, and the court must still appoint them.

Open slide 11

12. Twelve words, in plain language. (2 of 2)

The next six words. Letters of office are the court's certificate that a person has authority to act for an estate. Some routes never need them, and counsel decides. A deed is a recorded document that transfers ownership of real estate. A legal description is the exact wording that identifies a parcel; copy it exactly from the recorded deed, because an address is not a legal description. A P I N is the parcel number Cook County uses, and every one is searched separately. A lien is a recorded claim for a debt; report it, but do not conclude money is owed. A certified copy is issued by the office that keeps the record.

Open slide 12

13. Four kinds of information. Never blur them.

Four kinds of information, never to be blurred. A lead is a hint that points to something to check, like an obituary that lists four children. A recorded fact is a saved record with its source and date, like the death certificate showing the date of death. An unresolved question is something you need and do not have, or two sources that disagree. A conclusion for counsel is a legal result, like who the heirs are and their shares. Only counsel or the court settles those. A blank field is honest. A guess in a field looks like a fact.

Open slide 13

14. Every task follows eight beats.

Every substantive task follows eight beats. Why: know why the fact matters. Where: the best source. Ask: who to ask and what to say. Enter: exactly what goes in the field. Evidence: what proves it and where you save it. Verify: a second check. Done: evidence obtained and checked, not just a box ticked. And escalate: when to stop and ask. This example is the date of death. Done means the scan is saved and compared to the typed date. If the deed and the certificate show different dates, escalate.

Open slide 14

15. Check yourself: Module A.

Check yourself. Marcus tells you his mother's house has to go through probate, and asks you to write probate required in the route box. What do you do next? Pause and think. The answer: do not write it. You cannot choose or confirm a route. Note what Marcus said as a lead, leave the route unanswered, and explain kindly that a lawyer decides which path fits once the facts are gathered. Full probate is one possible outcome, not the starting assumption.

Open slide 15

16. Finding a potential case

Module B. Finding a potential case. You will learn four research channels. For each one we say what to look for, where to look, what the result establishes, what it does not establish, what to save, and what to do next.

Open slide 16

17. Four ways to find a lead, and what each one can tell you.

There are four ways to find a lead. Neighborhood observation can show a property that looks unoccupied, but not that the owner died or who owns it. Obituaries give a date of death and family names, but not that she owned property or that the survivors are the whole family. Recorder, assessor and treasurer records show who the records name as owner and what is recorded, but a tax bill is not proof of ownership. The probate office shows whether a case is already open. A vacant looking property, an obituary, a tax bill or a shared surname is a lead, never proof.

Open slide 17

18. Channel 1: walk and observe.

Channel one: walk and observe. Look for a property that appears unoccupied, such as overgrown yards, stacked mail, or notices on the door. Stay on the public sidewalk. Never go on the porch, into the yard, or into a mailbox. This establishes only that the property looks unoccupied today. It does not establish that the owner died, that it is abandoned, or who owns it. Save the address, date, time and a photo from the public way. Then look up the records before you do anything else. If you see danger, call the city, not the family.

Open slide 18

19. Channel 2: obituaries.

Channel two: obituaries. Look for a name, a date and place of death, the survivors named, and the funeral home. Save the page or a printout that shows the web address and the date you read it. An obituary establishes that someone published a notice and who they named. It does not establish legal relationship, the complete list of children, or that she owned property. A friend who is described as like a son is not an heir. The certified death certificate is the record of the date of death. Keep the obituary only as a lead.

Open slide 19

20. Channel 3: recorder, assessor and treasurer.

Channel three: recorder, assessor and treasurer. By P I N, find the recorded deed that put her on title, anything recorded after it, and the tax bill and its mailing address. Use the County Clerk's recordings search, and the assessor and treasurer sites. This establishes what the records show on the day you search. It does not establish that the owner died, who the heirs are, or that the deed is the last word on title. Search every P I N separately. Save a copy of each result with the P I N, the date searched, and the date the index runs through.

Open slide 20

21. Channel 4: the probate office and the Clerk.

Channel four: the probate office and the Clerk. Find out whether an estate case already exists for the person, and whether a lawyer is already acting. The Probate Division is at the Daley Center, Room twelve oh two, and the phone number was confirmed on the Clerk's site. This establishes whether the office can find a case under the names you give. A negative result is not a guarantee, because names may be spelled differently. Save every name spelling you tried, the date, and the result. If a case is open or a lawyer is acting, stop and escalate.

Open slide 21

22. Before you do more, check for existing proceedings.

Before you do more, check for existing proceedings. Is an estate case already open? Is a lawyer already helping? Was a will already filed? Is another family member already handling it? If you find an open case or a lawyer, stop. Do not duplicate work. Log the case number and the attorney of record, and escalate. Do not give advice to a lawyer's client. This order matters because work that duplicates or conflicts with a case already under way wastes the family's time and can cause confusion in court.

Open slide 22

23. A lead is not proof. Four examples.

A lead is not proof. A vacant looking house suggests the owner may have died or moved, but it does not prove death, abandonment or ownership. An obituary suggests a death and some survivors, but not the dates, the relationships or the full list of children. A tax bill suggests she was the taxpayer, but not that she owns the property. A shared surname suggests possible kinship, but not any legal relationship. Write the word lead or unconfirmed next to each until a record supports it.

Open slide 23

24. Save and name every source.

Save and name every source. Keep a lead log with one line per lead: the lead number, the date and your initials, the channel, what you saw or read in the source's own words, where the saved copy is, and a status. Use one file name pattern every time, starting with the lead number, then what it is, the parcel number and the date. Never rename a download to hide where it came from. The lead log is a handout, not part of the system. A lead you have not discussed with the family stays in your log.

Open slide 24

25. Check yourself: Module B.

Check yourself. You find an obituary for Delia Ann Marsh. The treasurer shows a tax bill in the name Marsh Delia A for the same address. A neighbor says her four kids want to sell. Which can you write as a recorded fact, and what is your next step? Pause. The answer: none are recorded facts yet. All three are leads. Search the recorded deed for each P I N and save the result with the date. Check for an existing estate case. Only then approach the family. Log all three leads with their sources.

Open slide 25

26. Respectful family outreach and consent

Module C. Respectful family outreach and consent. You will learn how to introduce yourself in person, by phone and in writing, how to speak with a grieving family, and how to get and record permission before you collect anything.

Open slide 26

27. Say four things, every time.

Say four things every time. One, who we are: a nonprofit that is not a law firm and is not part of any court or government office. Two, what we offer: with permission, we help families gather facts and papers about a loved one's property so a lawyer can review them. Three, what we cannot promise: legal advice, a lawyer, free help, who owns the property, or any result. Four, how to say no: the family can decline, ask you to stop, or ask you to delete what you have, at any time. If you are unsure whether you may say something, leave it out.

Open slide 27

28. In person: a short, gentle first visit.

In person, keep the first visit short and gentle. At the door say: Hello, I am from Second Chance Housing, a nonprofit. I am sorry to bother you. I am looking for family of Delia Marsh, who I understand may have passed. I am not selling anything and I am not a lawyer. May I leave my card, or tell you in two minutes why I am here? Go in daylight, stay on the public walk, and never touch or read mail or notices. Do not mention money or that the property is in trouble. If told no, do not come back again and again.

Open slide 28

29. By phone: ask permission to continue.

By phone, open by asking whether it is a good time, say who you are, and say you are sorry for their loss, if this is the right family. Then say we help families gather facts and papers so a lawyer can review them, that we are not a law firm, and ask whether you may ask a few questions and write down what they tell you. Confirm you have the right person before sharing anything about the property. Offer to call back or send a short email. On a voicemail, leave only your name, organization and number. Record the date, the time and what they agreed to.

Open slide 29

30. Written follow-up: email first, short, plain.

Written follow up. Use email, short and plain, and write only to an address the family gave you. Thank them, say you are sorry for their loss, repeat who we are, that we are not a law firm and cannot promise any result, and that they can stop or ask you to delete what you have at any time. Offer to send a short consent form to read and sign. Our rule is email for ordinary communication. Mail is for legal notices. Keep every message free of case details you have not confirmed with them.

Open slide 30

31. Speak with grief in the room.

Speak with grief in the room. Say: I am so sorry for your loss. Tell me about her, if you would like, and then listen. We can go as slowly as you need, and we can stop for today. Avoid phrases like she lived a long life, she is in a better place, or you need to act fast. Do not say you know how they feel. Do not rush to the forms. Expect tears, anger, silence and disagreement. You are not a counselor. If someone is in crisis, stop and follow your supervisor's safety steps.

Open slide 31

32. Confirm who they are, then ask for permission in writing.

Confirm who they are, then ask for permission in writing. Ask how they are related to the person who died. Do not accept a stranger's claim; check a name against the death certificate informant or family you have already confirmed. Ask to see identification only if your supervisor requires it. A signed consent should say what they permit you to collect, how it is stored and for how long, who it may be shared with, their preferred contact method, and how to withdraw consent. The sample form on the handout has not been approved by counsel, so use only the version your supervisor issues.

Open slide 32

33. What you must never do or say.

What you must never do or say. Never imply that you are a court, county, city or government worker. Never promise that counsel will be free, that any lawyer will take the case, that the family owns the property, or that any result is guaranteed. Never pressure anyone, return after a no, enter a property or go on private grounds without an invitation, collect or open mail, use a dead person's logins or cards, ask for payment, or discuss the case with anyone the family has not authorized. If you slip, correct it plainly and tell your supervisor the same day.

Open slide 33

34. Check yourself: Module C.

Check yourself. At the door Terrence says he will talk, but does not want his brothers and sisters told anything yet, and asks you to email him. Then he asks, will this be free? What do you do? Pause. The answer: write down that he wants email contact only and does not want others told yet, and do not share what he tells you without his permission. On the question, say you cannot promise that, you are not a law firm, and a lawyer decides what fees apply. Offer the consent form, with the sharing choices, and let him decide.

Open slide 34

35. Initial intake and urgent issues

Module D. Initial intake and urgent issues. You will learn the order of the first interview, what to ask about, how to spot an urgent problem, how to preserve a notice, and how to escalate without giving legal advice.

Open slide 35

36. The first interview, in a sensible order.

The first interview, in a sensible order. First, consent: review and sign it, and only then collect. Second, the person who died: full name, other names, date and place of death, and where she lived. Third, the property: address, every parcel number you know, who lives there, who pays what. Fourth, the paper that exists: will, trust, deed, death certificate, notices and bills, and who holds each original. Fifth, the family. Sixth, what they want: keep the home, share it, sell or refinance. That is their goal, not your advice. Plan more than one sitting.

Open slide 36

37. What to collect at intake.

What to collect at intake. The property: address, every parcel number, who lives there and what rent is paid. The person who died: legal name, other names, date and place of death, county of residence, marital status. Documents: death certificate, deed, will or trust, and any transfer on death instrument. Existing proceedings. The family: spouse or partner, every child including adopted children, children from earlier relationships and any who died, and where each person is. Money: insurance, taxes, mortgage, debts, accounts and funeral costs. Collect only what the family agrees to share, and record where each fact came from.

Open slide 37

38. The urgent-issues screen: ask these every time.

The urgent issues screen. Ask these every time. Any tax sale or delinquency notice? Any foreclosure or lender letter? Is the insurance current, and is there any cancellation notice? Are there dangerous conditions? Is there any lawsuit, court date or lawyer letter? Each one can carry a fixed date. Photograph the notice, note every date on it, and escalate the same day. The system has an urgent deadlines box and a calendar with reminder emails, but a date you do not enter will not remind anyone.

Open slide 38

39. Preserve the notice. Escalate the same day.

Preserve the notice and escalate the same day. Why: a notice carries hard dates, and a lost or unread notice can cost the family its rights. Where: from the family's own papers, with permission, never from the mailbox. Ask when it arrived and who else has seen it. Enter the notice type and every date exactly as written in the urgent deadlines box. Save photos of both sides and the envelope. A second person reads the date from the photo. Done means your supervisor has acknowledged the escalation. Never tell the family what the notice means.

Open slide 39

40. What you say when it is urgent.

What you say when it is urgent. Thank them for showing you the notice. Say it looks important and that you want a lawyer to see it quickly. Say you are not able to tell them what it means or what to do, but that you are sending it to your supervisor today. Ask permission to keep a photo and the best number to reach them in the next day. You may give published free legal help contacts. You may not interpret the notice, say it is fake or nothing to worry about, or tell them to pay, sign, respond or ignore it.

Open slide 40

41. Check yourself: Module D.

Check yourself. During intake, Naomi's niece hands you an envelope that says the house goes up for tax sale in a few months. She asks, is it okay to just wait until the lawyer is ready? Pause. The answer: do not answer the question. Say it looks time sensitive and you are escalating it today. Photograph both sides with her permission, enter the dates exactly as printed into urgent deadlines, have a second person compare them, and call your supervisor now. Say you cannot tell her whether waiting is safe, and give published free legal help contacts.

Open slide 41

42. Establishing ownership and possible routes

Module E. Establishing ownership and possible routes. You will learn to read a deed, to search for later recorded documents on every parcel, why heirs, authority, title and insurance are four different questions, and which routes counsel may evaluate. You never choose the route.

Open slide 42

43. Read the recorded deed, line by line.

Read the recorded deed line by line. Who is the grantee, the new owner? In our case the deed says Delia A. Marsh, which differs slightly from the death certificate. How is title held? Alone, or with words like joint tenants, trust, L L C or T O D I? Those mean different rules, and counsel decides what they mean. Copy the legal description word for word. List every parcel number. Record the recording stamp, document number and date. Reading a deed is manual. Do not interpret what a phrase means. Copy it, and write the question.

Open slide 43

44. Search every PIN for later documents.

Search every P I N for later documents. A deed is only the start. A later deed, mortgage, release, lien or transfer on death instrument can change everything. Search one P I N at a time on the clerk's recordings site and note the date the index runs through. Ask the family whether she signed anything after buying. Enter each document's number, type, date and parties. Save a result for each. A second person confirms every P I N on the property card appears in the list of P I Ns searched. The system blocks ready if one is missing.

Open slide 44

45. Four different questions, four different answers.

Four different questions with four different answers. Who are the heirs? A court or counsel answers that from the evidence you gather. Who may administer, meaning who has authority to act for the estate? The court appoints that person; counsel petitions. What does the record show about title? Counsel answers from your searches. What will a title insurer accept? The title company, in writing. Do not merge them. Knowing the heirs does not give anyone authority to sign for the estate, and a recorded deed does not say who inherits.

Open slide 45

46. Routes counsel may weigh. You do not pick.

Routes counsel may weigh. You do not pick. Independent administration may fit when someone needs authority to act and there is no dispute. Supervised administration means court oversight. A heirship order only may fit when the missing piece is a court finding of who the heirs are. Summary administration is limited by statute: the gross real and personal estate must not exceed one hundred thousand dollars, and every heir must consent in writing. Survivorship, a trust or a transfer on death instrument can pass title without an estate case. A title company may accept evidence in writing instead of letters. Counsel decides.

Open slide 46

47. &ldquo;No will&rdquo; does not mean &ldquo;probate is required.&rdquo;

No will does not mean probate is required. It can mean that title passes by survivorship because a joint owner is alive, that a recorded transfer on death instrument controls, that a trust holds the property, or that she never owned it alone. It can also mean the route is a heirship order, summary administration, a title company route, or administration. You may not write probate required. A small estate affidavit covers personal property, not a deed. Who inherits when there is no will depends on the whole family, which is why Module F collects every spouse, child and earlier death.

Open slide 47

48. Check yourself: Module E.

Check yourself. Your recorder search for the second parcel returns a deed to Delia A. Marsh and Robert Marsh, as joint tenants. Robert's death certificate shows he died in two thousand eleven. The family says, so it was all hers. What do you record? Pause. The answer: record exactly what you found, the document number, the parties and the words as joint tenants. Save Robert's certified death certificate. Do not write she owned it alone, and do not choose a route. Add an unresolved question for counsel about what effect the joint tenancy and Robert's death have on that parcel.

Open slide 48

49. Building the family evidence

Module F. Building the family evidence. You will record every relationship and death fact, learn the difference between someone who died before the decedent and someone who died after, and keep a register of unresolved questions. You record evidence. Counsel confirms who the legal heirs are.

Open slide 49

50. Do not start with four names. Start with a blank tree.

Do not start with four names. Start with a blank tree. For the person who died, ask about every spouse or civil union partner, ever. Ask about every child, biological or adopted, from any relationship, living or not. Ask about any child's children if the child has died. Ask who died, when, and whether it was before her. For each person record the full legal name, any other names, the relationship and how you know it, whether living, deceased or unknown, and contact details if the person agrees. If four names were assumed complete, a fifth child or a later spouse could be missed.

Open slide 50

51. Died before her, or died after her? The date changes everything.

Died before her, or died after her? The date changes everything. A person who died before the decedent cannot inherit, and that person's own descendants may stand in; counsel decides. A person who survived her and died later still survived her, so their share is part of their own estate. Record both dates of death and who they left behind. If a whereabouts is unknown, record what you tried, the dates and who you asked. If survival at her death is unknown, you cannot conclude either way. Leave it unresolved and write the question.

Open slide 51

52. Minor, disability, guardian: record facts, do not label.

Minor, disability, guardian: record facts, do not label. Record a person's age, with a date of birth from a record, if under eighteen. Record that a person has a disability if the family says so, as a lead. Record whether a court has appointed a guardian, and the case number if given. Do not conclude that a disability means the person is legally incapacitated. It does not. Do not conclude that a parent or sibling may sign for them, or that a minor can sign a waiver. Answer no to able to act for themselves only for a minor or someone with a guardian or court finding, and add the facts in the note.

Open slide 52

53. The family tree for the practice case.

Here is the family tree for the practice case. At the top, Delia Ann Marsh, born in nineteen forty one, who died on March fourteenth, two thousand twenty six. Her husband Robert Marsh died in two thousand eleven, and a certified record is still needed. Her four children are Marcus, the informant and proposed administrator; Renee, who lives in Atlanta; Terrence, who refuses to sign; and Naomi, whose address is unknown. This is a drawing. In the real case each box links to its evidence, and you add a box for anyone you learn of.

Open slide 53

54. The unresolved-questions register: make doubt visible.

The unresolved questions register makes doubt visible. For each question write a number, the question itself, why it matters, who can answer, how to find out, and the status. Where does Naomi live now? Is there a twenty twenty three marriage to Lamar Greene? Is Delia A the same person as Delia Ann? Does the two thousand nine mortgage still exist? Keep every question until it has an answer and a source. Write a question rather than a guess. Counsel reads this register first. The system has notes and checks, but no single register, so keep this sheet as a handout.

Open slide 54

55. Branches: spouse, name and a later death.

Three branches. A possible surviving spouse: a church certificate names Lamar Greene, but the death certificate says widowed. Record both documents with their sources, and the question. Do not conclude that she did or did not remarry. A name difference: the deed says Delia A and the certificate says Delia Ann. Record both names and supporting records like a marriage license and an identification. Do not conclude they are the same person; the heir picks the name to use, and counsel decides if more is needed. A later death: Renee died after her mother. Record her certified death certificate and who her heirs are. Do not conclude who receives her share.

Open slide 55

56. Check yourself: Module F.

Check yourself. Marcus gives you four names. Then Renee says Mom had a fifth child, Darnell, who died in a car crash in twenty nineteen and left a daughter. The obituary does not mention him. What do you record? Pause. The answer: record Darnell as a lead, with the date and who said it. Do not add or delete anyone from the heirs list as a conclusion. Add an unresolved question. Ask for his certified death certificate, his daughter's name and address, and a record of the relationship. Keep the heirs list not complete. Do not say the shares have changed. That is counsel's decision.

Open slide 56

57. Gathering documents and financial facts

Module G. Gathering documents and financial facts. You will learn the difference between an original, a copy and a certified copy, what to collect, who may request each record, what to look up before the first appointment, and what must wait until a court appoints someone.

Open slide 57

58. Original, copy, certified copy.

Original, copy and certified copy. The original is the signed document itself, such as a will or a signed deed. Never mark, staple or write on it, and note who holds it. A copy is a photocopy or scan for your working file. It is not proof of authenticity. A certified copy is issued and certified by the record custodian, such as a death certificate from the county. A notary stamp on a photocopy does not make it certified. Who may request a record, what identification is needed, and the fee all change, so read the office's current page on the day you order. This course does not state fees or times.

Open slide 58

59. The documents to gather, and where each comes from.

The documents to gather and where each comes from. Certified death certificates from the county vital records office; you need several, and you need them first. The recorded deed and every later document for each P I N, from the clerk's recordings. The will, trust or transfer on death instrument, from whoever holds it. Find these early, because the original matters. Name and relationship records, only if names differ or relationships are questioned. A photo identification for the proposed administrator, as private evidence. The system's documents box tracks each one's status, scan and where the paper original is kept.

Open slide 59

60. Taxes, mortgage, liens: look up each PIN.

Taxes, mortgage and liens: look up each parcel. Unpaid taxes, mortgages and liens affect what the family can do. Use the county treasurer for tax bills by parcel, the lender for a statement, and the recordings for recorded liens. Enter taxes owed, mortgage or lien details, and any notice dates. Verify that the tax bill, the lender statement and the recorded document agree on the parcel and the owner name. A lien in the records is a fact to report, not a conclusion that money is owed. For the two thousand nine mortgage, ask the lender for a payoff or release letter with written permission, and ask counsel what it means.

Open slide 60

61. Accounts, funeral costs, creditors and possible Medicaid recovery.

Accounts, funeral costs, creditors and possible Medicaid recovery. With permission, collect statements for sole name accounts, with only the last four digits of any account number, vehicles, other property, and unclaimed property from the state's search. 84. A worksheet is not a court form.

A worksheet is not a court form. The opening documents, if counsel chooses administration. The cover sheet tells the clerk the type of case, and is prepared by you for counsel. Proposed orders, notice and after filing items. A proposed order declaring heirship is what the judge may sign. You draft it and leave the signature and entry date blank. Second person review: compare, do not just count. A good review is done by someone other than the preparer. 97. What happens after counsel files

Module K. What happens after counsel files. You will see the whole journey from filing to a recorded deed and a closed estate, how the route changes it, and which parts are yours, which need an appointed representative, and which belong to counsel, the court or another professional.

Open slide 97

98. The journey on one page.

The journey on one page. One, counsel files, the clerk accepts it and assigns a case number. Two, a judge and hearing date are set. Three, heirs, and legatees if there is a will, are notified, and creditors later. Four, the hearing, and if the route needs it, the court appoints a representative and letters issue. Five, administration: an estate account, insurance, rent, taxes, creditor notice, inventory and claims. Six, distribution; for real estate, a recorded instrument. Seven, the recorded deed, which you confirm by searching the recordings by parcel number. Eight, closing the estate, a separate step. Other routes skip steps. Dates and fees are set by counsel and the court.

Open slide 98

99. Your route decides which steps exist.

Your route decides which steps exist. Independent administration needs letters once appointed. Heirs are notified, creditors by publication and mail, and the representative acts with less court supervision, though an interested person may ask for supervision. Supervised administration needs letters and the court approves more steps. A heirship order only needs no letters. The court declares heirship, there is no representative, and so no inventory or creditor notice. Summary administration may excuse letters. The title company route is the insurer's own requirements, in writing. Do not require letters on a route that proceeds without them.

Open slide 99

100. Creditors, inventory and claims: dates that run.

Creditors, inventory and claims: dates that run. For creditor notice, the representative publishes once a week for three successive weeks, and mails notice to each known creditor. The claims date is at least six months from the first publication, or three months from mailing, whichever is later. Claims not filed by then are barred, within the limits of the statute. Do not state a bar date to anyone. Counsel does. The representative files a verified inventory within sixty days after letters issue. You can compile the asset facts you already collected, and the representative verifies. The system's calendar gives estimates only.

Open slide 100

101. Two milestones: the recorded deed, and the closed estate.

Two milestones: the recorded deed, and the closed estate. For an independent representative who has not sold the real estate, the statute requires an instrument that contains the legal description and releases the estate's interest, recorded and delivered to the person entitled. The representative signs it before a notary, with the transfer tax exemption and stamps, and it is recorded at the clerk's recordings. You confirm with a search of every parcel number. Closing the estate is a separate step, with an accounting and a verified report. Never tell a family the house is done because the estate closed, or the estate closed because the deed recorded.

Open slide 101

102. Who does what, after filing.

Who does what, after filing. Attending the hearing and ruling on the route belong to counsel and the court. You remind the family of the date if asked. Notifying heirs and creditors and publishing is directed by counsel and signed and sent by the representative; you gather addresses and keep tracking and receipts. Opening an estate account, getting an employer identification number and collecting rent happen only once someone is appointed, as counsel confirms. Signing the deed is done by the representative before a notary. Title insurance questions are decided by the title company. If it needs a signature as the estate, an account in the estate's name or a legal judgment, it is not yours.
